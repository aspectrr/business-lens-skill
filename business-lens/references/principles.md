# Business Decision Lenses — Core Principles

The seed of this skill. Durable, repeatedly-validated ideas from the sources
Collin trusts. When mentoring a decision, apply the relevant lens first, then dig
for depth in the source material via the search scripts.

> Accuracy note: paraphrased principles, not verbatim quotes. When a specific claim
> matters, confirm against the source (book-search / yt-search / web-search / web_fetch).

## Paul Graham — How to start and think (Collin's #1 source)

PG's lens is the default frame for almost any early-stage question.

- **Make something people want.** The single most important thing. A startup that
  makes something lots of people want, and can reach them, wins. Everything else
  is secondary.
- **Do things that don't scale.** Recruit your first users manually. Delight them
  personally. Hand-hold. The unscalable early work is what gets you the users that
  make scaling possible. (Collin already lives this — handwritten notes, gifts,
  personal attention.)
- **Default alive, not default dead.** Know whether you can reach profitability on
  your current trajectory with current spending. "Ramen profitable" — enough revenue
  to cover the founders' living costs — changes everything; it removes the clock.
- **Talk to users.** Watch them use the product. The feedback that reshapes a product
  comes from watching real use, not from surveys of opinions.
- **Live in the future, then build what's missing.** The best startup ideas aren't
  searches for ideas; they're things you notice are broken or absent because you're
  closer to a problem than other people. Notice what annoys you.
- **Specific beats general.** Write for humans. Build the smallest thing that solves
  the real problem. A small, specific, obsessed user base beats a large indifferent one.
- **Schlep blindness.** We avoid hard, unglamorous problems. The valuable ones are
  often the schleps nobody else wants to do.

## Alex Hormozi — Offers, growth, and money (books + YouTube)

Use when the question is about pricing, offers, acquisition, or scaling revenue.

- **Make a Grand Slam Offer.** An offer so good people feel stupid saying no. Get
  there by stacking value (dream outcome, perceived likelihood of success, speed to
  result) and removing friction (effort, time, sacrifice/risk), not by discounting.
- **The Value Equation** (core of $100M Offers):
  `Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement × Time to Outcome⁻¹) −
  (Effort & Sacrifice × Time to Value⁻¹ × Risk)`. Raise the numerator, cut the denominator.
- **Retention before acquisition.** Don't pour fuel (leads) on a leaky bucket. Fix the
  product, onboarding, and churn first; doubling down on acquisition before retention
  is set just burns money faster.
- **Content → Audience → Leads → Sales.** The modern acquisition chain. Content builds
  an audience; the audience becomes leads; leads convert. Move people up a value ladder
  (free → low-ticket → core → high-ticket).
- **The content/audience matrix** (the inbound model Collin referenced):
  - *One-to-one* — sales calls, DMs. High touch, doesn't scale. Use early.
  - *One-to-many* — content, broadcasts, webinars. The scaling engine.
  - *Many-to-one* — inbound leads from content (many find you, you respond to each).
  - *Many-to-many* — community, UGC, referrals. Hardest but stickiest.
  The lead/inbound type you run dictates which model fits.
- **Make / Grow / Manage / Deploy.** The four money skills ($100M Money Models): make it,
  grow it (sales/marketing), manage it (operations/finance), deploy it (reinvest).
  Most founders over-index on one and neglect the others.

## David Ogilvy — Advertising & persuasion

Use when the question is about messaging, positioning, ads, or copy.

- **Research first.** You can't write good copy without knowing the product, the
  customer, and the competition inside out. Do the homework before the headline.
- **The Big Idea.** Without a big idea, your advertising passes like a ship in the night.
  Find one distinct, memorable idea and build everything around it.
- **The headline is 80 cents of the dollar.** If the headline doesn't pull them in, the
  body copy is never read. Write dozens; pick the best.
- **The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife.** Respect the reader's intelligence.
  Don't insult, don't condescend, don't lie.
- **Long copy sells** — when the reader is interested. Give enough information to make
  the decision. Brevity for its own sake loses sales.
- **Test everything.** Headlines, offers, media. Let data settle arguments.

## Charlie Munger — Thinking & avoiding self-destruction (Poor Charlie's Almanack)

Use when the question is about judgment, risk, or avoiding catastrophic error.

- **Invert, always invert.** "Tell me where I'm going to die, so I never go there."
  Instead of asking "how do I succeed?", ask "how do I guarantee failure?" and avoid that.
- **Show me the incentive.** "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."
  People's behavior follows incentives, not stated intentions — including your own.
- **Avoid stupidity.** Easier and more reliable to avoid doing dumb things than to be
  brilliant. The path to being consistently right is removing the ways you're wrong.
- **A latticework of mental models.** Don't reason from one discipline. Use models from
  psychology, economics, physics, biology, math. Big decisions need multiple lenses.
- **Circle of competence.** Know what you know and what you don't. Play inside your circle;
  the size matters less than knowing its boundary.

## Rob Fitzpatrick — Customer truth (The Mom Test)

Use whenever validating an idea, a price, or a feature with a customer.

- **Ask about their life, not your idea.** "How do you currently deal with X?" beats
  "Would you use Y?" People are bad at predicting their own future behavior.
- **Opinions are noise; behavior is signal.** A compliment about your idea is worthless.
  A commitment of time, money, reputation, or a next meeting is real data.
- **Talk about specifics and the past.** Generic claims about the future are lies people
  tell to be nice. Concrete past actions and current workflows are the truth.

## How to use these lenses

1. Name the decision or question plainly.
2. Identify which lens applies (product → PG; pricing/offer → Hormozi;
   messaging → Ogilvy; judgment/risk → Munger; customer validation → Mom Test).
3. State what the lens implies.
4. Stress-test it: what would the *opposite* advice be, and why is it wrong here?
   (Munger inversion.)
5. Convert to one concrete next action.
